You’ve seen “No Added Sugar” on protein bars, desserts, drinks and snacks. It sounds simple, but the phrase tells you less than you might think. Here’s what it actually means, what allulose is, and how to read a label in about 30 seconds.

Short answer: “No Added Sugar” means no sugar was added during production. The product can still contain natural sugars from fruit or milk, and it isn’t automatically low in calories or carbohydrates.

What does “No Added Sugar” really mean?

No added sugar is not the same as sugar free. A product can contain naturally occurring sugars from ingredients like fruit or milk without having any sugar added while it was made.

It also isn’t automatically low in calories or carbohydrates. The label tells you something about how the product was made, not its whole nutritional value.

Meet allulose: a different kind of sugar

Allulose is a rare sugar found naturally in small amounts in foods such as figs and raisins. It tastes much like regular sugar, but your body processes it differently.

  • It has about 0.4 calories per gram, compared with 4 calories per gram for regular sugar.
  • On its own, it adds very little to blood glucose.
  • It gives sweetness and helps create the texture and browning we expect from baked desserts.

That’s why allulose has become a popular ingredient in modern baking.

What does the latest research say?

A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis looked at controlled human trials of allulose and another rare sugar, tagatose.

The researchers found that allulose could reduce the rise in blood glucose and insulin after eating, in the settings they studied. They did not find convincing evidence that allulose improves body composition, blood lipids or long-term blood sugar control.

So allulose is a useful alternative to traditional sugar. It isn’t a miracle ingredient. Larger amounts can also cause digestive discomfort in some people.

Is a no-added-sugar dessert automatically healthier?

Not necessarily. A dessert with no added sugar can still be high in calories or low in nutritional value. Instead of judging it by one claim, look at the whole product:

  • What ingredients does it contain?
  • How much protein and fiber does it provide?
  • What are the calories and total carbohydrates per serving?
  • What is being used to make it sweet?

A better choice comes from understanding the whole food, not one attractive phrase on the packaging.

How to read the label in 30 seconds

  1. Check Total Sugars and Added Sugars. They are different numbers. Natural sugars can still be there when Added Sugars is zero.
  2. Look at the ingredient list. Ingredients are listed by weight, from highest to lowest, so it shows you what you’re mostly eating.
  3. Look beyond sugar. Check serving size, calories, protein, fiber and total carbohydrates. If you monitor your blood glucose, these details matter even more.

Frequently asked questions

Is allulose an artificial sweetener?

No. Allulose is a naturally occurring rare sugar, although commercial allulose is generally produced through controlled processing.

Does allulose raise blood sugar?

On its own, it has little effect on blood glucose. Research also suggests it may reduce post-meal glucose responses in certain settings, but results depend on the overall meal and the individual.

Does no added sugar mean zero carbohydrates?

No. Foods can still contain carbohydrates from flour, fruit, milk and other ingredients.

Is allulose safe for everyone?

It is permitted for use in foods in the United States. Large amounts may cause digestive discomfort, and research into long-term effects is still developing.
